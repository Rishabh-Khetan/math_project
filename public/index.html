The games used here are a simplified, illustrative dataset of eight Big 12 teams. They are not the real schedule or results.

How the linear system Ax = b produces the ranking

Each team i gets an unknown rating ri. With n teams there are n unknowns, and the Colley method supplies n linear equations, one per team. In matrix form the system is

C r = b

(the same shape as Ax = b, with C as the coefficient matrix and r as the unknown vector).

Where the equations come from

Let wi and li be the wins and losses of team i, and ti = wi + li its games played. A simple starting estimate is ri = (1 + wi) / (2 + ti). Writing wi = (wi − li)/2 + ti/2, and replacing the average opponent rating of 1/2 by the sum of the actual ratings of the opponents faced, gives one equation per team:

(2 + ti) ri − Σj ∈ opponents of i rj = 1 + (wi − li) / 2

Collecting these equations gives the matrix entries:

  • Cii = 2 + ti (diagonal)
  • Cij = −(number of games between teams i and j) (off-diagonal)
  • bi = 1 + (wi − li) / 2

C is symmetric and strictly diagonally dominant, so it is invertible and the system has exactly one solution. Solving it (here with NumPy’s linalg.solve) gives the rating vector r; sorting the entries from largest to smallest gives the ranking. Each rating lies between 0 and 1, and the ratings always sum to n/2, which is used below as a check.
